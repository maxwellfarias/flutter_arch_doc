## Corrected and Organized Transcript

## 1. Introduction

As a manager, I could get rid of almost any individual employee I wanted. This was the most brutally honest interview I have done so far.

I believe most Performance Improvement Plans, or PIPs, are a combination of dishonesty and psychological unreality.

This is Ethan Evans. He went from being fired twice to becoming a Vice President at Amazon, where he led a team of more than 800 engineers.

After being fired twice, he realized something important: “Who is the common element in all of this? It is me.”

In this interview, I asked him about his experience working with famous Amazon executives such as Jeff Bezos and Andy Jassy. Ethan said he had probably been in around 50 meetings with Jeff and another 50 with Andy.

I also asked him about the behind-the-scenes reality of performance management at Amazon. What prevents a manager from portraying an employee unfairly? According to Ethan, people may not like hearing the answer, but he is willing to tell the truth.

---

## 2. Ethan’s Career Before Amazon

Ethan said he never expected to become a Vice President at Amazon. His career evolved naturally over time.

He originally wanted to be an engineer. He grew up when home computers were just beginning to appear. Before email existed, he wrote a physical letter to Apple asking how he could work there. Apple replied that, at the time, they only hired engineers from five universities: Stanford, Caltech, Berkeley, Carnegie Mellon, and MIT.

Ethan was from Ohio, so California felt far away. He did not get into MIT, but he did attend Carnegie Mellon. His original plan was to eventually work at Apple.

Instead, he earned a master’s degree at Purdue and then began working at startups, mostly in areas related to high-speed networking.

His career path included:

- A physical-layer networking company.
- An internet search company that was acquired by Lycos.
- A network management company working with large networks.
- Internet advertising and audio streaming.
- A short detour at a company that made entertainment lighting for dance clubs.
- Amazon, where he was hired to help build Prime Video based on his experience with music streaming.

He explained that there was a chain connecting each step of his career, even though the startups themselves were very different. Most were small, fast-growing companies. Many of them failed or struggled around the dot-com crash.

That was also where Ethan was made redundant twice. Both cases, he said, were partly caused by the fact that he was abrasive.

---

## 3. Choosing Startups Over Big Companies

When asked why he chose startups early in his career, Ethan said the decision was initially driven by his friends.

Some of his friends had joined a startup founded by former Carnegie Mellon professors. They talked about how exciting it was and introduced him to stock options, which were new to him at the time.

His college roommate joined the company before it went public and became a millionaire in his mid-twenties. Ethan joined later and did not make as much, but he still earned enough to make a down payment on a house at around age 25.

He admitted that his early career choices were not deeply intentional. He followed his friends first. Only later did he begin thinking more deliberately about what a career should look like.

---

## 4. Being Fired Twice and Learning From It

Ethan said he did not see the layoffs coming, although people had tried to warn him.

In his first performance review, his manager wrote that Ethan’s solution to some problems seemed to be: “Let’s knock their heads together.” Ethan described himself as combative and arrogant at the time.

He used to joke that people called him a “loose cannon” only because the cannon was pointed at them. In reality, he was often telling people how wrong they were.

As he rose into leadership roles, his technical ability and willingness to jump into difficult projects helped him advance. However, his conflict with peers created problems.

At one startup, he argued with a product leader who said the company’s product goals were unrealistic. Ethan saw the product leader as lacking commitment, but later realized the product leader had been right.

At another startup, Ethan led engineering while the sales VP was promising customers features the company had not yet built. Ethan objected because the product could not do what sales was promising. Later, he realized that the company was desperate: signing the contract was possibly the only way to avoid going out of business.

In that case, Ethan was technically laid off, but he was the only person laid off. He said that although the company called it a layoff, he considers it being fired.

After this happened twice, he asked himself who the common element was. The answer was himself.

That realization caused him to examine what he needed to change. His technical skills were not the problem. His ability to work well with others was.

He began studying workplace psychology, motivation, communication, and leadership. He learned to ask questions, listen, and avoid escalating conflicts.

---

## 5. Soft Skills, Assertiveness, and Being “Strategically Annoying”

Ethan said that if he had combined his early energy with better soft skills, that would have been ideal.

He still believes people need to be somewhat pushy in their careers. He likes the phrase “strategically annoying.” To him, this means having opinions, advocating for them, and refusing to be passively compliant, but doing so without becoming aggressive or disrespectful.

Earlier in his career, Ethan argued in unhealthy ways. He raised his voice, became visibly angry, and used phrases such as “that is a dumb idea.”

He believes he got away with this for a while because he was often right and because he was driven. However, being right was not enough. The way he delivered his message damaged relationships.

The lesson is not to avoid disagreement. The lesson is to learn how to disagree effectively.

---

## 6. Corporate Politics and Building Alliances

Ethan explained that when someone is loud, abrasive, and confrontational, people may stop disagreeing with them directly. Instead, they begin talking about them behind their back.

He caused some of his own political problems because people eventually thought, “This person is impossible to deal with. Let’s see if we can get him removed.”

Another mistake was that he did not build enough alliances. If he strongly disagreed with one person, he did not have strong enough relationships with others who could warn him or support him.

Many engineers believe that being right is what matters most. Ethan learned that relationships, trust, and how people feel about working with you matter just as much.

Sometimes organizations choose a technically worse solution because it feels safer or more comfortable to the people involved.

---

## 7. Joining Amazon

Ethan first learned about Amazon while working at Lycos, which had a partnership with Barnes & Noble. Barnes & Noble wanted to compete online, and Ethan’s job involved understanding why they were losing to Amazon.

Through that work, he saw how far ahead Amazon was compared with traditional brick-and-mortar companies.

When an Amazon recruiter later contacted him, Ethan could explain that he already understood Amazon as a competitor and had been impressed by its strategy.

At the time, Amazon was already a public company with around 10,000 employees. Compared with the small startups Ethan had worked at, Amazon seemed large and stable.

---

## 8. Promotion to Director at Amazon

Ethan joined Amazon as a senior manager and was later promoted to director.

At the time, Amazon was growing rapidly. The expectations for a director were that the person could lead a business or major effort independently, needing only high-level goals.

Ethan’s promotion came from a combination of good luck and a bold decision.

He took on a risky project involving a partnership with TiVo. His leadership advised against doing it, but Ethan believed in the project. He told his senior vice president that unless he was explicitly ordered not to do it, he would proceed.

The project succeeded. In his promotion case, the key line was that without Ethan, the TiVo partnership would not have happened.

This became evidence that he could make high-judgment decisions and lead important business initiatives.

Ethan also advocated for himself. He told his manager that his career was very important to him and that he needed to know whether it was equally important to Amazon. He did not threaten aggressively, but the message was clear: if Amazon did not support his growth, he would need to consider his options.

He believes this is an example of raising a difficult topic politely and strategically.

---

## 9. The Reality of Promotions

Ethan said that, as a leader, he sometimes had two equally qualified people on his team. If one person strongly pushed for promotion and threatened to leave, while the other silently waited, the person pushing often received attention first.

He acknowledged that this may feel unfair. However, managers often operate under short-term constraints. They may only have the time, political capital, or business need to promote one person in a given cycle.

His point was not that this is ideal, but that it is often how organizations really work.

The silent hard worker may believe that good work alone will be rewarded, but Ethan warned that this is not always true. People need to make their ambitions known.

---

## 10. Taking Risks to Grow

Ethan emphasized that rapid career growth often requires taking risks.

The TiVo project could have damaged his reputation if it had failed. Because he pushed for it against leadership advice, he would have owned the failure.

However, taking calculated risks can move a career forward faster than always playing it safe.

Ethan said that one of his career regrets is not taking more risks. If he could do his career over again, he would probably take even more.

---

## 11. Promotion to Vice President and the “Magic Loop”

By the time Ethan was working toward becoming a Vice President, he had been at Amazon for a long time and understood the internal process much better.

He developed an idea he calls the “magic loop.” The concept is to work closely with your manager to understand what they need, then create a partnership:

> “I will do everything you need done. Your job is to make sure I am rewarded for it.”

The deal is that the employee goes above and beyond, but there must be a clear payoff.

His manager eventually told him he would try to get Ethan promoted to Vice President. It took about two and a half years for the manager to be willing to put his credibility behind Ethan’s promotion.

Ethan said managers are not usually trying to withhold promotions. As a Vice President, he wanted more strong directors because it made his own job easier. However, employees still have to demonstrate that they are ready.

---

## 12. A Failed Promotion Promise

Ethan shared a story about a talented young engineer who helped ship the first version of a product. Ethan promised to help promote him from SDE I to SDE II.

However, Ethan was new to Amazon and did not understand the promotion process. Amazon only ran promotions during specific cycles, and he missed the deadline.

The engineer eventually left for another large technology company and had a successful career, but Ethan said his mistake probably cost the engineer a year.

The lesson is that employees should understand the promotion process themselves. Even if the manager is acting in good faith, they may not know what they are doing.

Employees should ask specific questions such as:

- What is the deadline?
- When will the promotion document be written?
- Who needs to approve it?
- What evidence is required?
- What happens if the cycle is missed?

Ethan emphasized that although the manager is responsible, it is the employee’s career that is at risk.

## 13. Twitch Integration and Leading Without Authority

After becoming a Vice President, Ethan moved into a role involving Twitch after Amazon acquired the company.

Amazon had a process for major acquisitions. A Vice President would act as an integration liaison to help the company get value from the acquisition.

Ethan did not become the CEO of Twitch’s boss. Emmett Shear remained in charge. Ethan’s role was to advise, influence, and help integrate Twitch into Amazon.

He chose this role deliberately because it challenged his soft skills. Previously, he had led a global team of 800 people. In the Twitch role, he had no direct authority. He had to influence the CEO and leadership team without giving orders.

This was a major leadership challenge, especially because Twitch was a younger, venture-funded company with a very different culture.

---

## 14. Leadership Gaps in Fast-Growing Startups

Ethan said Twitch had several leadership gaps, which is normal in fast-growing startups.

One major issue was profitability. Twitch had been venture-funded and was spending heavily. Some Twitch leaders initially believed profitability mattered less because Amazon had acquired them and had significant financial resources.

Ethan’s job was to help move Twitch toward profitability while also respecting its culture.

Another challenge was that early employees do not always scale with the company. Founders often feel loyalty to the people who helped build the company, but some of those people may not be ready to lead at the next level.

This meant Emmett Shear had to learn the difficult lesson of hiring senior leaders above some early employees or moving people out of roles they had previously held.

---

## 15. Jeff Bezos vs. Andy Jassy

Ethan said he had spent dozens of hours in meetings with both Jeff Bezos and Andy Jassy.

The biggest difference, in his view, is that Jeff was the founder. As the founder, Jeff could take risks with the company that others would not take.

Ethan recalled a budget discussion where the CFO tried to slow Jeff down by reminding him that Amazon only had so much money in the bank. Jeff reportedly replied by asking how much money that was, because he might want to spend it.

To Ethan, this showed that Jeff saw his role as deciding how to allocate resources boldly.

Andy Jassy, by contrast, was described as a more classic business leader. He had to operate within a different kind of responsibility because Amazon was not originally his company.

Ethan said both Jeff and Andy demanded a lot from leaders. However, he personally felt more inspired by Jeff. Jeff made him feel emotionally supported once he was on board. Andy, in Ethan’s experience, asked more probing questions and made him feel more personally accountable for the outcome.

---

## 16. Amazon Gaming and the Problem of “Zombie Products”

Ethan discussed Prime Gaming and Amazon’s gaming ambitions. He said that many projects can hit their formal goals while still failing against the larger vision.

For example, a team may ship features, hit milestones, and meet financial targets, yet still fail to become strategically important.

He said Amazon wanted to become a major player in gaming, possibly comparable to Tencent, but Amazon Gaming and Prime Gaming remained relatively niche.

Ethan described some big-company products as “zombie products.” These are products that are not clearly succeeding, but no one wants to shut them down because doing so would be an admission of failure.

As a result, they continue operating without a clear path to becoming truly significant.

---

## 17. Goals, Incentives, and Reality

Ethan explained that there is often tension between the goals that matter and the goals leaders want to be measured against.

For example, when leading the Amazon Appstore, the real strategic goal was to compete with Google on Android. But Google Play came preinstalled on Android phones, while Amazon’s app store required users to install it manually.

Ethan did not know how to win under those conditions. For his performance review and compensation, he wanted goals he could achieve. For the good of the company, the important goals were much harder and possibly impossible.

This creates a common problem in large companies: teams may optimize for measurable goals rather than the goals that would truly matter.

---

## 18. Stack Ranking, PIPs, and Performance Management

Ethan described Amazon’s approach to performance management, including unregretted attrition.

Unregretted attrition means employees the company is willing, or even wants, to lose. Ethan described it as a nicer way of saying that some percentage of employees are expected to leave, whether by quitting, being pressured out, or being fired.

He said Amazon once removed this goal after receiving criticism. However, managers then stopped having difficult conversations with low performers. Without pressure, many managers avoided performance management altogether.

As a result, the goal returned.

Ethan believes most PIPs are ineffective because by the time a manager creates one, the manager has often already mentally decided that the employee should leave.

He said that people often contact him after being placed on a PIP at Amazon and ask for help surviving it. His advice is usually to use that time to look for another job, because very few people successfully recover from that stage.

He acknowledged that some managers may start PIPs with good intentions. However, the system often creates pressure to remove the employee, especially when managers have attrition targets.

---

## 19. Can a Manager Fire Anyone They Want?

Ethan clarified a statement he had written: a manager can usually get rid of any one employee they want, but they cannot get rid of everyone.

If a manager creates a pattern of pushing many people out, the organization may eventually notice. However, for a single employee, the manager has a major advantage.

The manager usually tells the story first. They can frame the employee’s work positively or negatively using the same facts.

For example, an engineer who performs many code reviews could be described in two ways:

- Positive narrative: the engineer improves everyone’s code and raises the team’s quality.
- Negative narrative: the engineer spends too much time reviewing others’ work and does not produce enough original work.

The same behavior can be interpreted in opposite ways.

Ethan’s point is that software engineering performance is not as objective as people often believe. Storytelling and framing matter.

Bad managers may eventually get caught if they repeat abusive behavior. But a clever manager who wants to remove one person may have enough power to do so.

---

## 20. HR Is Not There to Rescue You

Ethan warned employees not to assume HR will investigate and rescue them from a bad manager.

HR’s role is primarily to protect the company. If an employee complains after the manager has already built a negative narrative, HR may hear the complaint as similar to what underperforming employees often say.

That does not mean every complaint is false. It means the system often favors the manager because the manager has status, authority, and the first opportunity to frame the situation.

Ethan’s advice is simple: if you have a vindictive or difficult boss, do not enter a direct fight unless you have real leverage. Instead, try to repair the relationship, transfer to another manager, or leave.

---

## 21. Final Career Advice

When asked what advice he would give his younger self, Ethan gave two answers.

First, he would continue choosing high-growth environments. He compared a career ladder to an escalator. If you work at a company that is growing rapidly, the organization itself creates upward movement.

Amazon grew enormously while Ethan was there, and that growth created opportunities.

Second, he would learn much earlier that work is still about human beings. Expertise and being right are valuable, but relationships, reputation, communication, and trust are equally important.

He encouraged people to build a reputation and make connections. Even introverts can do this through tools such as LinkedIn, writing, and online communities.

Amazon called Ethan for the job, not the other way around. He said people should build the kind of reputation that makes opportunities come to them.

# Key Lessons

## Career Growth

- Choose high-growth environments whenever possible.
- Do not rely only on hard work to be promoted.
- Make your ambitions clear to your manager.
- Learn the promotion process yourself.
- Take calculated risks.

## Leadership

- Being right is not enough.
- Relationships matter as much as logic.
- Learn to disagree without damaging trust.
- Build alliances before you need them.
- Influence is more powerful than authority at senior levels.

## Performance Management

- PIPs are often a sign that the company has already decided to remove the employee.
- Managers have significant power to shape the narrative around performance.
- HR usually protects the company, not the individual employee.
- Employees should not wait passively if their manager is hostile or unfair.

## Communication

- Be “strategically annoying,” not abrasive.
- Ask questions instead of only making statements.
- Raise difficult topics politely and clearly.
- Build a reputation before you need opportunities.

# Short Summary

Ethan Evans’ career shows that technical ability can help someone rise quickly, but poor communication and weak relationships can eventually derail that progress. After being fired twice, he realized that his abrasive style was the common problem. He changed by studying workplace psychology, soft skills, and leadership.

At Amazon, he learned how promotions, performance management, executive decision-making, and corporate politics really work. His central advice is that career growth requires a combination of skill, strategic risk-taking, reputation-building, relationship management, and clear self-advocacy.
